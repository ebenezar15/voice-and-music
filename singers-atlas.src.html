Now sounding
A complete guide, beginner to advanced

Every voice is an instrument you can learn to play.

Your voice has a power source (breath), a vibrator (vocal folds) and a resonator (throat, mouth and nose). Learning to sing is learning to coordinate those three, then training your ear so the notes land where you mean them to.

Ten rules every good singer follows

  1. Warm up first. Gentle trills and hums before songs, every time.
  2. Breathe low, stand tall. Ribs expand; shoulders stay still.
  3. Listen before you sing. Hear the note in your head, then sing it.
  4. Never push through pain. Hoarseness or a scratchy feeling means stop.
  5. Record yourself. What you hear inside your head is not what others hear.
  1. Hydrate all day. Water you drink now helps your folds hours later.
  2. Volume is not power. Resonance, not shouting, makes a voice carry.
  3. Practise slowly. Speed and runs come from accurate slow repetition.
  4. Sleep and rest. Muscles and tissue repair when you rest.
  5. Tell the story. Technique serves the meaning of the words.
The instrument

How the voice works

Singing is controlled air. The lungs push air up, the vocal folds chop it into puffs hundreds of times a second, and the spaces above shape that buzz into vowels and tone. Click any part of the diagram.

Inhale: diaphragm drops, lungs fill.

The three systems

  • Power · lungs, diaphragm, ribs, abdominals. Supplies steady air pressure.
  • Source · the larynx and vocal folds. Turns airflow into vibration (pitch).
  • Filter · pharynx, mouth, nose. Shapes the buzz into vowels and tone colour.

Inside the larynx: the vocal folds

The vocal folds (often called vocal cords) are two small bands of muscle covered in a soft, flexible layer, about 1.25–2.5 cm long. When you breathe they open into a V. When you sing they close, and air pressure makes them vibrate. Higher notes stretch and thin them; lower notes let them shorten and thicken.

The animation is slowed down roughly 50×. At A3 your folds really open and close 220 times every second; at the top of a soprano's range, over 1,000 times.

Sound in three numbers

Pitch

Frequency (Hz)

How fast the folds vibrate. A4 is 440 vibrations per second. Doubling the frequency raises the note one octave.

Loudness

Amplitude (dB)

How much air pressure and fold closure you use. Healthy loud singing comes from resonance and efficient closure, not more force.

Tone colour

Timbre (overtones)

Every note contains overtones above the main pitch. Which ones are strongest decides whether a voice sounds dark, bright, breathy or ringing.

The language singers read

Music basics for singers

You do not need to read music fluently to sing well, but knowing notes, intervals, scales and rhythm makes everything faster: learning songs, talking to musicians and practising exercises.

The keyboard is a map of pitch

Twelve notes repeat in every octave: seven white keys (C D E F G A B) and five black keys (sharps ♯ and flats ♭). The step between any two neighbouring keys is a semitone. Two semitones make a whole tone. Middle C is C4. Click any key.

Tip: the number after the letter is the octave. C4 to B4 is one octave; C5 starts the next.

Intervals: the distance between two notes

Songs are built from intervals. Learning to recognise them by ear is the single biggest step towards singing in tune. Each one has a famous melody that starts with it, which makes it easy to remember.

Solfège: singing names for notes

Many choirs and teachers use Do–Re–Mi so the same syllable always means the same scale step, whatever key you are in.

Numbers mean the same thing: an exercise written 1-3-5-3-1 means Do-Mi-Sol-Mi-Do.

Keys, chords and harmony in one minute

Key

The home note a song settles on. Changing the key moves the whole song up or down, which is how singers fit a song to their range.

Chord

Three or more notes at once. A major triad (1-3-5) sounds bright; a minor triad (1-♭3-5) sounds darker.

Harmony

Singing a different note that fits the chord. Thirds above the melody are the most common backing-vocal harmony.

Classification

Voice types, female and male

Voice type is decided by more than range. Teachers also listen to where your voice feels most comfortable (tessitura), where it changes gear (passaggio) and its natural colour (timbre). Classifying too early can push you into the wrong repertoire, so treat this as a starting point.

Range versus tessitura. Range is every note you can produce. Tessitura is where your voice sits comfortably for long stretches. A song that keeps returning to the top of your range will tire you even if you can hit every note. Choose keys by tessitura.
Voices change. Male voices drop roughly an octave during puberty and settle by the early twenties. Female voices change too (lower slightly after puberty and again after menopause). Training also extends range at both ends.
Pitch accuracy

How to sing in tune

Singing in tune is a skill, not a gift. Fewer than 2% of people have true tone deafness (amusia). Most off-pitch singing comes from not hearing the note clearly first, tension, or weak breath support, and all three can be trained.

Common pattern: going flat

Usually low energy, a heavy or dark vowel, or tiredness. Fix: brighter vowel, keep the soft palate lifted, think "up" as melodies descend, support through the end of the phrase.

Common pattern: going sharp

Usually excitement, too much air pressure or tension on high notes. Fix: less push, more space, steady airflow, release the jaw and tongue.

Common pattern: scooping

Sliding up into every note from below. Fix: audiate first, aim for the top of the note, practise staccato onsets on single pitches.

Foundation

Posture and breathing

A balanced body lets the breath move freely and keeps the larynx unblocked. Breath "support" means controlling how fast air leaves, so the folds get steady pressure, not a blast or a trickle.

Six-point posture check, head to feet

Sitting to sing? Sit towards the front of the chair, both feet flat, spine tall as if standing from the waist up. Never slump against the backrest.

Three ways people breathe

Avoid

Clavicular (high chest)

Shoulders and collarbones lift. Shallow, tense, and it squeezes the neck muscles around the larynx.

Partial

Chest only

Upper ribs move but the belly is held in. Better, but you run out of air quickly.

Aim for

Low, rib-and-belly (costal-diaphragmatic)

Belly, sides and back expand all around the waist. Shoulders stay still. Gives the most air and control.

Quick test: Put one hand on your upper chest and one on your belly, then sniff in slowly. The lower hand should move first and most. Lie on your back with a book on your stomach if you need to feel it.

Breathing pacer

Ready

On the exhale, hiss on "sss" at an even volume. If the hiss gets louder or weaker, your support is wobbling. Over weeks, increase the exhale count while keeping it even.

Appoggio: the support balance

Classical teachers call it appoggio ("leaning"). After inhaling, the muscles that breathe in (diaphragm, outer intercostals) keep gently resisting while the muscles that breathe out (abdominals, inner intercostals) slowly push. The ribs stay wide for as long as possible. The result is steady, controllable air pressure.

Too little support

Breathy tone, flat pitch, phrases die away.

⇄

Balanced

Steady tone, free throat, ribs stay open, belly moves in slowly.

⇄

Too much push

Pressed, loud, sharp; neck veins out; hoarse afterwards.

The break and how to bridge it

The passaggio is where one register hands over to the next. Untrained voices "crack" or "flip" here because chest voice is carried up too heavily, then suddenly lets go. The fix is to start thinning the folds and narrowing the vowel before the break.

1 · Lighten

Approaching the break, use less weight and volume. Think "lift off", not "push up".

2 · Narrow the vowel

"Ah" turns slightly towards "uh", "ay" towards "eh", "ee" towards "ih". This is vowel modification.

3 · Use semi-occluded sounds

Lip trills, "ng", straw phonation and "gee" sirens through the break train the coordination with less strain.

Resonance: hear how the mouth shapes a vowel

Your folds make the same buzz for every vowel. The shape of your tongue, jaw and lips creates resonant peaks called formants that turn the buzz into "ee", "ah" or "oo". Press a vowel below: the same synthetic buzz is passed through different formant filters.

F1 rises as the jaw opens ("ah" highest). F2 rises as the tongue moves forward ("ee" highest). Trained singers also develop the singer's formant, a cluster near 2,500–3,500 Hz that lets an unamplified voice ring over an orchestra.

Placement and resonance spaces

Chest resonance

Felt as vibration in the sternum. Adds warmth and depth on lower notes.

Mask (forward) resonance

Buzz around the nose, cheeks and lips. Adds brightness and carry. Hum "mm" to feel it.

Pharyngeal space

An open throat, like the start of a yawn. Adds roundness and depth.

Nasal resonance

A lowered soft palate sends sound through the nose. Useful for "m", "n", "ng"; too much on vowels sounds whiny.

Natural vibrato is a regular pitch wave of about 5–7 cycles per second, roughly a quarter to half a semitone wide. It appears on its own when breath and throat are free. A slow, wide wobble or a fast, nervous bleat both signal a problem.

Maintenance

Keeping your voice healthy

Vocal folds are living tissue that collide hundreds of times a second. They need water, rest and gentle use, like any athlete's muscles. Most voice problems come from overuse, dehydration and poor technique, and most are preventable.

Do

  • Drink water steadily through the day (pale urine is a good sign).
  • Steam with plain hot water or a personal steamer for 10 minutes when dry or tired.
  • Warm up before singing and cool down after (gentle hums and sirens downwards).
  • Take vocal naps: 10 minutes of silence for every hour of heavy voice use.
  • Sleep 7–9 hours. Tired voices go flat and crack.
  • Use a microphone rather than shouting over a band.
  • Keep the air humid (40–60%) in dry or air-conditioned rooms.
  • Manage reflux: avoid big meals and spicy food within 3 hours of bed.

Avoid

  • Smoking and vaping (dry and irritate the folds; smoking raises cancer risk).
  • Throat clearing and harsh coughing. Swallow or sip water instead.
  • Whispering to "rest" the voice; it can strain more than soft speech.
  • Shouting at matches, concerts or in noisy bars.
  • Singing through pain, hoarseness or laryngitis.
  • Too much alcohol and caffeine without extra water.
  • Medicated throat lozenges with menthol before singing; they numb warning signals.
  • Singing outside your range for long stretches to copy a recording.
